### B. Percentage multipliers

- Increase by `p%` → multiply by `(100 + p)/100`.
- Decrease by `p%` → multiply by `(100 − p)/100`.
- `x% of y = y% of x`.

**Example:** `16% of 25 = 25% of 16 = 4`.

### C. BODMAS

Always solve in this order:

1. Brackets
2. Orders/powers
3. Division and multiplication, **left to right**
4. Addition and subtraction, **left to right**

### D. Squares worth memorising

`1²` to `20²`:

`1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100, 121, 144, 169, 196, 225, 256, 289, 324, 361, 400`

Also remember: `25²=625`, `30²=900`, `40²=1600`, `50²=2500`.

### E. The base-100 method

When no absolute amount is given and only a relative percentage or ratio is required, choose a convenient reference base—often 100. It is a working scale, not the actual unknown total.

# 1. Profit, Loss and Discount

## Why this is first

It was the strongest Maths pattern: **4–6 questions in every one of the 40 retained papers**.

## Recognise it

Look for: bought, cost, sold, gain, loss, marked price, discount, shopkeeper, faulty weight, successive discount.

## Core formulas

```text
Profit = SP − CP
Loss   = CP − SP

Profit% = Profit / CP × 100
Loss%   = Loss / CP × 100

SP at p% profit = CP × (100 + p) / 100
SP at p% loss   = CP × (100 − p) / 100

CP = SP × 100 / (100 + profit%)
CP = SP × 100 / (100 − loss%)   [for loss% below 100]

Discount = MP − SP
Discount% = Discount / MP × 100
SP after d% discount = MP × (100 − d) / 100
```

### Rule that prevents most mistakes

- Profit/loss percentage is normally calculated on **CP**.
- Discount percentage is calculated on **MP**.

## Type 1 — Direct profit or loss

**Example:** An item costs ₹800 and is sold at 15% profit. Find SP.

```text
SP = 800 × 115/100 = ₹920
```

**Reverse example:** An article is sold for ₹920 at 15% profit. Find CP.

```text
CP = 920 × 100/115 = ₹800
```

## Type 2 — Successive discounts

Two discounts are applied one after the other, not added directly.

```text
Equivalent discount = d₁ + d₂ − (d₁d₂/100)
```

**Example:** MP = ₹1,500; discounts = 20% and 10%.

Method 1 — multipliers:

```text
SP = 1500 × 80/100 × 90/100 = ₹1,080
```

Method 2 — equivalent discount:

```text
20 + 10 − (20×10/100) = 28%
SP = 1500 × 72/100 = ₹1,080
```

**Trap:** `20% + 10% ≠ 30%` because the second discount is applied to the reduced price.

## Type 3 — Markup followed by discount

Use multipliers.

**Example:** CP = ₹800. A seller marks it 25% above CP and gives 12% discount. Find profit%.

```text
MP = 800 × 1.25 = 1000
SP = 1000 × 0.88 = 880
Profit = 880 − 800 = 80
Profit% = 80/800 × 100 = 10%
```

**One-line method:**

```text
Net multiplier = 1.25 × 0.88 = 1.10 → 10% profit
```

## Type 4 — Change in selling price changes profit percent

If increasing SP by ₹`x` changes profit from `p%` to `q%`:

```text
CP = x × 100 / (q − p)
```

**Example:** An extra ₹90 changes profit from 20% to 35%.

```text
15% of CP = 90
CP = 90 × 100/15 = ₹600
```

## Type 5 — Equal SP, one gain and one loss

If two articles are sold at the **same SP**, one at `x%` gain and the other at `x%` loss:

```text
Overall loss% = x²/100
```

**Example:** Same SP; 20% gain on one and 20% loss on the other.

```text
Loss% = 20²/100 = 4%
```

This shortcut works only when both SPs are equal, the gain/loss rates are equal, and `0 ≤ x < 100`.

## Type 6 — False weight

If a seller charges the cost of 1,000 g but gives only `w` g:

```text
Gain% = (1000 − w) / w × 100
```

**Example:** Gives 800 g as 1 kg at the cost-price rate.

```text
Gain% = 200/800 × 100 = 25%
```

## Common traps

- Calculating profit% on SP instead of CP.
- Adding successive discounts.
- Forgetting that “marked 30% above CP” means `MP = 130% of CP`.
- Using the equal-SP shortcut when the selling prices are not equal.
- Ignoring the weight shortage before applying markup or discount.

> **20-second recap:** CP is the base for profit/loss; MP is the base for discount. Convert every change into a multiplier and multiply in order.

# 2. Percentage

## Recognise it

Look for: percent, increase/decrease, income, expenditure, savings, population, votes, marks, pass/fail, consumption, price change.

## Core rules

```text
x% of y = x/100 × y
Percentage change = Change / Original × 100
New value after p% increase = Old × (100+p)/100
New value after p% decrease = Old × (100−p)/100
```

### Successive percentage change

For two increases `a%` and `b%`:

```text
Net increase% = a + b + ab/100
```

For an increase `a%` followed by a decrease `b%`:

```text
Net change% = a − b − ab/100
```

A negative result means an overall decrease.

## Type 1 — Successive change

**Example:** A value rises by 20%, then falls by 10%.

```text
100 → 120 → 108
Net increase = 8%
```

Formula:

```text
20 − 10 − (20×10/100) = 8%
```

**Trap:** +20% and −20% do not cancel.

```text
100 → 120 → 96 = 4% decrease
```

## Type 2 — “More than” and “less than”

**Example:** A is 25% more than B.

Assume `B = 100`, so `A = 125`.

B is less than A by:

```text
25/125 × 100 = 20%
```

### Fast conversion

If A is `p%` more than B, then B is less than A by:

```text
100p / (100+p) %
```

If A is `p%` less than B, then B is more than A by the following amount, for `0 ≤ p < 100`:

```text
100p / (100−p) %
```

## Type 3 — Income, expenditure and savings

Always use:

```text
Income = Expenditure + Savings
```

**Example:** Income is ₹20,000. Expenditure is 75% of income. Find savings.

```text
Expenditure = 75% of 20,000 = 15,000
Savings = 20,000 − 15,000 = ₹5,000
```

**Example without actual income:** A person spends 80% of income. Income rises 25%, while expenditure rises 10%. Find percentage change in savings.

Assume old income = 100.

```text
Old expenditure = 80; old saving = 20
New income = 125
New expenditure = 80 × 1.10 = 88
New saving = 125 − 88 = 37
Increase in saving = 17
Increase% = 17/20 × 100 = 85%
```

## Type 4 — Election/votes

Use the chain:

```text
Population or voters → votes cast → valid votes → candidate share
```

**Example:** 80% of 10,000 voters vote. Of these, 5% are invalid. A gets 60% of valid votes.

```text
Votes cast = 10,000 × 0.80 = 8,000
Valid votes = 8,000 × 0.95 = 7,600
A's votes = 7,600 × 0.60 = 4,560
```

**Trap:** Candidate percentage is often of valid votes, not total voters.

## Type 5 — Population or repeated growth

**Example:** Population = 20,000; grows 10% each year for 2 years.

```text
New population = 20,000 × 1.10² = 24,200
```

## Type 6 — Price and consumption

If price rises and expenditure must stay unchanged:

```text
Required reduction in consumption%
= Increase% / (100 + Increase%) × 100
```

**Example:** Price rises 25%.

```text
Reduction = 25/125 × 100 = 20%
```

If price falls by `p%`, expenditure stays unchanged, and `0 ≤ p < 100`, the possible increase in consumption is:

```text
p / (100−p) × 100%
```

## Common traps

- Dividing by the new value instead of the original value.
- Treating successive changes as simple addition.
- Confusing total votes, votes cast and valid votes.
- Forgetting that savings are the difference between income and expenditure.

> **20-second recap:** Choose the correct base, convert changes to multipliers, and build percentage chains one step at a time.

# 3. Simple and Compound Interest

## Recognise it

Look for: principal, rate, annual, amount, interest, compounded, half-yearly, instalment, loan, debt.

## Simple Interest

```text
SI = P × R × T / 100
Amount = P + SI
```

**Example:** ₹2,000 at 6% simple interest for 3 years.

```text
SI = 2000 × 6 × 3 / 100 = ₹360
Amount = ₹2,360
```

### Reverse SI questions

From `SI = PRT/100`:

```text
P = 100×SI / (R×T)
R = 100×SI / (P×T)
T = 100×SI / (P×R)
```

## Compound Interest

For compound interest, use a rate and period count that refer to the **same compounding period**:

```text
Amount = P(1 + i)ⁿ
CI = Amount − P
```

Here `i` is the decimal rate per compounding period and `n` is the number of those periods. For annual compounding, `i = R/100` and `n` is the number of years.

**Example:** ₹5,000 at 10% compound interest for 2 years.

```text
Amount = 5000 × 1.10² = ₹6,050
CI = 6,050 − 5,000 = ₹1,050
```

### Different rates in different years

Multiply each year's factor.

**Example:** ₹10,000 grows by 10% in year 1 and 20% in year 2.

```text
Amount = 10,000 × 1.10 × 1.20 = ₹13,200
```

### Half-yearly compounding

```text
New rate per period = R/2
Number of periods = 2T
```

**Example:** 12% per year, compounded half-yearly for 1 year:

```text
Rate = 6% per half-year; periods = 2
Amount = P × 1.06²
```

## Difference between CI and SI

For exactly 2 years with one unchanged annual rate and annual compounding:

```text
CI − SI = P(R/100)²
```

**Example:** P = ₹10,000, R = 10%, T = 2 years.

```text
Difference = 10,000 × (0.10)² = ₹100
```

For exactly 3 years with one unchanged annual rate and annual compounding:

```text
CI − SI = P[3(R/100)² + (R/100)³]
```

## Equal instalments — use a timeline

Do not guess an instalment formula. Move every payment to the same date.

### Compound-interest example

A loan is cleared by two equal year-end instalments of ₹605 at 10% compound interest. Find the loan.

Bring both payments back to today:

```text
Loan = 605/1.10 + 605/1.10²
     = 550 + 500
     = ₹1,050
```

### Simple-interest example

A principal of ₹1,000 is borrowed today at 10% simple interest per annum and is cleared by equal payments at the ends of years 1 and 2. Compare all values at the end of year 2.

At the end of year 2:

```text
Loan value = 1000(1 + 10×2/100) = 1200
First payment x grows for 1 year → 1.10x
Second payment x is paid then     → x
2.10x = 1200
x = ₹571.43 approximately
```

## Common traps

- Using CI formula for SI or vice versa.
- Forgetting to subtract principal when the question asks only for CI.
- For half-yearly compounding, changing the rate but not the number of periods.
- Comparing instalments at different dates.
- Treating “amount” as “interest.”

> **20-second recap:** SI grows by the same amount each year; CI grows on the new amount. For instalments, put all values on one date.

# 4. Speed, Distance, Trains and Boats

## Core triangle

```text
Distance = Speed × Time
Speed = Distance / Time
Time = Distance / Speed
```

## Unit conversion

```text
km/h to m/s → multiply by 5/18
m/s to km/h → multiply by 18/5
```

**Example:** `72 km/h = 72 × 5/18 = 20 m/s`.

## Average speed

### Equal distances

If equal distances are travelled at speeds `u` and `v`:

```text
Average speed = 2uv / (u+v)
```

**Example:** Equal distances at 40 and 60 km/h.

```text
Average = 2×40×60 / 100 = 48 km/h
```

### Equal times

```text
Average speed = (u+v)/2
```

**Trap:** Do not use the ordinary average for equal distances.

### General rule

```text
Average speed = Total distance / Total time
```

This rule always works. “Total time” means total elapsed time, including any stoppage that the question says to include.

## Relative speed

- Same direction → relative speed = `|u−v|`.
- Opposite directions → relative speed = `u+v`.

**Example:** Two runners move in the same direction at 12 m/s and 8 m/s. Relative speed = `4 m/s`.

## Trains

```text
Time = Total length to be crossed / Relative speed
```

- Train crossing a pole/person: distance = train length.
- Train crossing a platform: distance = train length + platform length.
- Two trains: distance = sum of train lengths.

**Example:** A 180 m train crosses a 120 m platform at 54 km/h.

```text
Speed = 54 × 5/18 = 15 m/s
Distance = 180 + 120 = 300 m
Time = 300/15 = 20 seconds
```

## Boats and streams

Let boat speed in still water = `b`; stream speed = `s`. For upstream travel, the boat must be faster than the stream: `b > s`.

```text
Downstream speed = b + s
Upstream speed   = b − s

b = (Downstream + Upstream)/2
s = (Downstream − Upstream)/2
```

**Example:** Downstream = 15 km/h; upstream = 9 km/h.

```text
Boat speed = (15+9)/2 = 12 km/h
Stream speed = (15−9)/2 = 3 km/h
```

## Race/chase

For a faster pursuer following a slower leader on the same route:

```text
Catch-up time = Initial gap / (Pursuer speed − Leader speed)
```

**Example:** A is 100 m ahead. B runs 2 m/s faster.

```text
Catch-up time = 100/2 = 50 seconds
```

## Common traps

- Mixing metres with kilometres or seconds with hours.
- Forgetting platform length.
- Adding speeds when objects move in the same direction.
- Using `(u+v)/2` for equal-distance journeys.
- Reversing upstream and downstream.

> **20-second recap:** Make units equal, decide total distance, decide relative speed, then use `time = distance/speed`.

# 5. Ratio and Proportion

## Ratio basics

`a:b` means `a/b`. Multiplying or dividing both parts by the same non-zero number does not change the ratio.

## Divide a total in a ratio

If total `S` is divided in `a:b`:

```text
First share  = S × a/(a+b)
Second share = S × b/(a+b)
```

**Example:** Divide ₹840 in `3:4`.

```text
First = 840×3/7 = ₹360
Second = 840×4/7 = ₹480
```

## Combine two ratios

**Example:** `A:B = 2:3` and `B:C = 4:5`.

Make B equal. LCM of 3 and 4 is 12.

```text
A:B = 8:12
B:C = 12:15
Therefore A:B:C = 8:12:15
```

## Proportion

If:

```text
a:b = c:d
```

then:

```text
a×d = b×c
```

## Mean, third and fourth proportional

```text
Mean proportional between a and b = √(ab)
Third proportional to a and b     = b²/a
Fourth proportional to a,b,c      = bc/a
```

**Examples:**

- Mean proportional between 4 and 9 = `√36 = 6`.
- Third proportional to 4 and 6 = `36/4 = 9`.
- Fourth proportional to 2, 3, 8 = `3×8/2 = 12`.

## Direct and inverse proportion

Use direct proportion only when `y/x` stays constant, and inverse proportion only when `x×y` stays constant, with all other relevant factors unchanged.

- In the book example below, cost per book is constant, so cost is directly proportional to quantity.
- In the worker example below, total work and worker efficiency are fixed, so workers and days are inversely proportional.

**Direct example:** 5 books cost ₹300. 8 books cost:

```text
300×8/5 = ₹480
```

**Inverse example:** 12 workers finish a job in 15 days. 20 equally efficient workers need:

```text
12×15 = 20×days
Days = 9
```

## Common traps

- Adding ratio parts to the total instead of using shares.
- Combining ratios without making the common term equal.
- Confusing direct and inverse proportion.
- Using `ab` instead of `√ab` for mean proportional.

> **20-second recap:** Convert ratios into equal units, make common terms equal, and use cross multiplication for proportions.

# 6. Average

## Master formula

```text
Average = Sum / Number of items
Sum = Average × Number of items
```

## Type 1 — Missing value

**Example:** Average of 5 numbers is 18. Four numbers total 64. Find the fifth.

```text
Total of 5 = 5×18 = 90
Fifth = 90−64 = 26
```

## Type 2 — Add or remove a member

**Example:** The average age of 8 students is 20 years. A ninth student aged 28 years joins.

```text
Old total = 8×20 = 160
New average = (160+28)/9 = 188/9 = 20 8/9
```

## Type 3 — Replacement shortcut

If one value is replaced, preserve the direction of the average change:

```text
New value − Old value = n × (New average − Old average)
```

**Example:** Average of 10 numbers rises by 2 when one number is replaced.

```text
New value − Old value = 10×2 = 20
The new number is 20 greater than the old number.
```

If the average falls, the right-hand side is negative, so the new value is smaller.

## Type 4 — Combined average

```text
Combined average = (n₁a₁ + n₂a₂) / (n₁+n₂)
```

**Example:** 20 students average 60 marks; 30 students average 70.

```text
Combined average = (20×60 + 30×70)/50
                 = 3300/50
                 = 66
```

## Type 5 — Consecutive equally spaced numbers

For an arithmetic sequence, the average is the middle value.

**Example:** Average of `11, 15, 19, 23, 27` is `19`.

For an even number of terms, average the two middle terms.

## Common traps

- Adding averages without considering group sizes.
- Forgetting that removal changes both sum and count.
- Using the replacement shortcut without multiplying by the number of items.
- Confusing average speed with the ordinary arithmetic mean.

> **20-second recap:** Find the hidden total first: `sum = average × count`. Almost every average problem becomes simple after that.

# 7. Number System and Simplification

## Divisibility rules

| Divisor | Quick test |
|---:|---|
| 2 | Last digit is even |
| 3 | Sum of digits is divisible by 3 |
| 4 | Last two digits are divisible by 4 |
| 5 | Last digit is 0 or 5 |
| 6 | Number is divisible by both 2 and 3 |
| 8 | Last three digits are divisible by 8 |
| 9 | Sum of digits is divisible by 9 |
| 10 | Last digit is 0 |
| 11 | Difference of alternating digit sums is 0 or a multiple of 11 |

**Example — divisibility by 11:** Is 2728 divisible by 11?

```text
(2+2) − (7+8) = 4−15 = −11
```

Yes, because −11 is a multiple of 11.

## HCF and LCM

For two positive integers:

```text
First number × Second number = HCF × LCM
```

**Example:** Product of two numbers = 540; HCF = 6; LCM = 90.

```text
HCF×LCM = 6×90 = 540
```

The relation is satisfied.

### Same-remainder questions

The **greatest** divisor that leaves the same unspecified remainder on several numbers is the HCF of their differences.

If the common remainder `r` is specified, find:

```text
HCF(number₁−r, number₂−r, ...)
```

**Example:** Find the greatest number that divides 65 and 89 leaving the same unspecified remainder.

```text
Difference = 89−65 = 24
Greatest possible divisor = 24
```

For three or more numbers with an unspecified common remainder, find the HCF of their differences. Other divisors of that HCF may also leave a common remainder, but the HCF gives the greatest one.

## Remainder basics

```text
(a+b) mod m = [(a mod m)+(b mod m)] mod m
(a×b) mod m = [(a mod m)(b mod m)] mod m
```

Do not calculate a huge number if only its remainder is needed.

## Unit-digit cycles

| Base ending | Cycle of unit digits | Cycle length |
|---:|---|---:|
| 0 | 0 | 1 |
| 1 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 | 2, 4, 8, 6 | 4 |
| 3 | 3, 9, 7, 1 | 4 |
| 4 | 4, 6 | 2 |
| 5 | 5 | 1 |
| 6 | 6 | 1 |
| 7 | 7, 9, 3, 1 | 4 |
| 8 | 8, 4, 2, 6 | 4 |
| 9 | 9, 1 | 2 |

**Example:** Unit digit of `7²³`.

Cycle length = 4.

```text
23 ÷ 4 leaves remainder 3
Third number in 7-cycle (7,9,3,1) = 3
```

For a **positive integer exponent**, if division by the cycle length leaves remainder 0, choose the last item in that cycle. For any non-zero base raised to exponent 0, the value—and therefore the unit digit—is 1.

## Simplification

- Cancel common factors before multiplication.
- Convert mixed fractions to improper fractions.
- Use BODMAS.
- Keep fractions until the end when decimals become messy.

**Example:**

```text
(36×25)/(18×5) = (36/18)×(25/5) = 2×5 = 10
```

## Common traps

- Applying the divisibility-by-4 rule to the whole number instead of its last two digits.
- Forgetting the zero-remainder case in unit-digit cycles.
- Confusing HCF with LCM.
- Solving left to right while ignoring BODMAS.

> **20-second recap:** Check divisibility from digits, use differences for same-remainder questions, and use cycles for powers.

# 8. Time and Work / Pipes

## Basic idea

If A finishes a job in `x` days:

```text
A's one-day work = 1/x
```

If A and B work together:

```text
Combined rate = 1/x + 1/y
Time = 1 / Combined rate
```

For two workers only:

```text
Together time = xy/(x+y)
```

## Fast LCM method

**Example:** A takes 12 days; B takes 18 days.

Take total work = LCM(12,18) = 36 units.

```text
A's rate = 36/12 = 3 units/day
B's rate = 36/18 = 2 units/day
Together = 5 units/day
Time = 36/5 = 7.2 days
```

## Efficiency and time

For the same fixed work completed at constant rates, efficiency is inversely proportional to completion time.

```text
Efficiency A : Efficiency B = Time B : Time A
```

**Example:** A takes 10 days; B takes 15 days.

```text
Efficiency A:B = 15:10 = 3:2
```

## Worker joins or leaves

Break the work into periods.

**Example:** A alone takes 10 days. A works for 4 days, then B completes the remaining work in 3 days. Find B's alone time.

```text
A does 4/10 = 2/5
Remaining = 3/5
B does 3/5 in 3 days → 1/5 per day
B alone needs 5 days
```

## Men–days–hours

For equally efficient workers operating at constant rates:

```text
Workers × Days × Hours per day ∝ Work
```

**Example:** 12 workers complete a job in 15 days. How many days for 20 workers?

```text
12×15 = 20×D
D = 9 days
```

## Pipes and cisterns

- Filling pipe = positive work.
- Emptying pipe/leak = negative work.

**Example:** Pipe A fills a tank in 6 h; a leak empties it in 12 h.

```text
Net rate = 1/6 − 1/12 = 1/12
Tank fills in 12 h
```

## Alternate-day work

Find work completed in one full cycle, then handle the remainder. Do not count a complete cycle past the finishing point: process the next worker in order and allow a fractional final day if the remaining work is less than that worker's one-day work.

**Example:** A does 1/6 per day, B does 1/12 per day; they work on alternate days starting with A.

```text
Two-day work = 1/6 + 1/12 = 1/4
Three full cycles = 3/4 work in 6 days
Day 7: A does 1/6 → total 11/12
Remaining = 1/12
B needs one full day for 1/12
Total = 8 days
```

## Common traps

- Adding completion times instead of work rates.
- Forgetting to subtract a leak's rate.
- Using the LCM as time instead of total work units.
- Ignoring which worker starts an alternate-day schedule.
- Applying inverse proportion when worker efficiencies differ.

> **20-second recap:** Convert days into one-day work, add positive rates, subtract negative rates, and use LCM units to avoid fractions.

# 9. 3D Mensuration — recent-priority topic

## Formula table

| Solid | Volume | Curved/Lateral surface | Total surface |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cube, side `a` | `a³` | `4a²` | `6a²` |
| Cuboid `l,b,h` | `lbh` | `2h(l+b)` | `2(lb+bh+hl)` |
| Cylinder | `πr²h` | `2πrh` | `2πr(h+r)` |
| Cone | `⅓πr²h` | `πrl` | `πr(l+r)` |
| Sphere | `4/3 πr³` | `4πr²` | `4πr²` |
| Hemisphere | `2/3 πr³` | `2πr²` | `3πr²` |

For a cone:

```text
Slant height l = √(r²+h²)
```

Diagonals:

```text
Cube diagonal = a√3
Cuboid diagonal = √(l²+b²+h²)
```

## Type 1 — Find a missing dimension

**Example:** Cylinder volume = `1,540 cm³`, radius = 7 cm, use `π=22/7`. Find height.

```text
1540 = 22/7 × 7² × h
1540 = 154h
h = 10 cm
```

## Type 2 — Melting and recasting

Material is conserved, so volumes are equal.

```text
Old volume = New volume
```

**Example:** A cube of side 6 cm is melted into cubes of side 2 cm.

```text
Number = 6³/2³ = 216/8 = 27
```

## Type 3 — Water displacement

The displaced-water volume equals the volume of the **submerged part** of the object. It equals the whole object's volume only when the object is fully submerged.

For a vertical-sided cuboid tank with no overflow:

```text
Submerged volume = Tank base area × Rise in water level
```

## Type 4 — Change in dimensions

If every linear dimension becomes `k` times:

```text
Area becomes k² times
Volume becomes k³ times
```

**Example:** Sphere radius doubles.

```text
Surface area becomes 2² = 4 times
Volume becomes 2³ = 8 times
```

## Common traps

- Using diameter as radius.
- Confusing curved surface area with total surface area.
- Forgetting the base in total surface area.
- Using area equality in a melting/recasting problem instead of volume equality.
- Forgetting `1 m³ = 1,000,000 cm³`.

> **20-second recap:** Draw the solid, mark radius/diameter, decide whether the question asks volume, curved area or total area, then substitute units carefully.

## 10A. 2D Mensuration

| Shape | Area | Perimeter/Circumference |
|---|---|---|
| Rectangle | `lb` | `2(l+b)` |
| Square | `a²` | `4a` |
| Triangle | `½ × base × height` | Sum of sides |
| Equilateral triangle | `√3/4 × a²` | `3a` |
| Parallelogram (adjacent sides `a,b`) | `base × height` | `2(a+b)` |
| Trapezium (`a,b` parallel; perpendicular height `h`) | `½(a+b)h` | Sum of sides |
| Circle | `πr²` | `2πr` |
| Semicircle | `½πr²` | `πr+2r` |

**Trap:** Perimeter of a semicircle includes the diameter.

### Path/border questions

- Outer area − inner area = border/path area.
- Draw both dimensions before subtracting.

**Example:** A uniform 2 m wide path runs all around the inside boundary of a `20 m × 14 m` rectangle.

```text
Inner dimensions = (20−4) × (14−4) = 16×10
Path area = 20×14 − 16×10 = 280−160 = 120 m²
```

## 10B. Mixture and Alligation

For two prices or concentrations measured on the same linear basis, and with `Cheaper < Mean < Dearer`:

```text
Cheaper quantity : Dearer quantity
= (Dearer − Mean) : (Mean − Cheaper)
```

If the desired mean lies outside the two component values, a positive mixture of only those two components is impossible.

**Example:** Mix rice at ₹30/kg and ₹50/kg to obtain ₹38/kg.

```text
Cheaper : Dearer = (50−38):(38−30) = 12:8 = 3:2
```

### Repeated replacement

If the mixture is homogeneous before every withdrawal, the same `x` litres are removed each time from total volume `V`, and the vessel is refilled to `V` with liquid containing none of the original component being tracked, then after `n` repetitions:

```text
Original liquid left = Initial amount × (1 − x/V)ⁿ
```

**Example:** From 40 L milk, 10 L is removed and replaced with water twice.

```text
Milk left = 40 × (1−10/40)²
          = 40 × (3/4)²
          = 22.5 L
```

## Common traps

- Reversing the alligation ratio.
- Applying one replacement only when the operation repeats.
- Forgetting that the vessel returns to full volume after each replacement.

> **20-second recap:** In alligation, take cross-differences. In replacement, multiply the surviving fraction once for each operation.

# 1. Coding-Decoding

## Recognise it

Look for: “in a certain code,” “written as,” “coded as,” “what is the code,” or two coded sentences with common words.

## Five-step code detector

1. Are the word and code the same length?
2. Test a constant forward/backward shift.
3. Test reversal.
4. Test different rules for odd and even positions.
5. If codes are numbers, test alphabet values, sums, products or positions.

Do not invent a complicated rule until simple rules fail.

## Type 1 — Same shift for every letter

**Example:** `CAT → DBU`. Find the code for `DOG`.

```text
C→D (+1), A→B (+1), T→U (+1)
D→E, O→P, G→H
Answer: EPH
```

## Type 2 — Different shift at each position

**Example:** A three-letter word is coded by `+1, +2, +3`. Code `BAD`.

```text
B+1 = C
A+2 = C
D+3 = G
Answer: CCG
```

Write the shift under each position; do not calculate mentally when signs alternate.

## Type 3 — Reverse, then change

**Example rule:** Reverse the word, then move every letter +1. Code `BAT`.

```text
BAT → TAB → UBC
```

Always perform operations in the stated or discovered order.

## Type 4 — Alphabet-value code

**Example:** Code value is the sum of letter positions. Find the value of `BAD`.

```text
B+A+D = 2+1+4 = 7
```

Possible number rules include:

- Sum of positions
- Difference of first and last
- Product of selected positions
- Number of letters, vowels or consonants
- Reverse alphabet positions

Confirm the rule on every given example.

## Type 5 — Common-code language

In this usual puzzle convention, assume each distinct word has one stable, distinct code token and token order is irrelevant, unless the question indicates otherwise. Compare the sets of words and code tokens.

Compare sentences and use common words.

```text
“red apple sweet” = ka mi po
“apple is fresh”  = mi tu na
```

The only common word is **apple** and the only common code is **mi**.

```text
apple = mi
```

### If two words are common

You may identify only a **set** of two possible words and a set of two possible codes until another sentence separates them. Do not force the individual pairings too early.

## Type 6 — Relabelling

If the question says “pen is called book, book is called bag…”, answer using the **new name**, not the real name.

**Example:** If water is called milk and milk is called juice, what do we drink to quench thirst?

Real object = water; coded name of water = milk.  
**Answer: milk.**

## Common traps

- Finding a rule that fits only one letter.
- Forgetting wrap-around after Z.
- Reversing after shifting when the rule requires shifting after reversal.
- Matching a common code without checking that the common word is unique.
- Answering with the real object in a relabelling question.

> **20-second recap:** Compare letter positions, test shift/reversal/alternation, and use intersections for sentence codes.

# 2. Series and Sequence

## Recognise it

Look for: next term, missing term, question mark, letter series, number series, or letters to fill several blanks.

## Number-series decision order

Check in this order:

1. First differences
2. Second differences
3. Multiplication or division
4. Multiply then add/subtract
5. Squares, cubes, primes or Fibonacci-type sums
6. Odd-position and even-position series separately

## Type 1 — Increasing differences

**Example:** `3, 8, 15, 24, 35, ?`

```text
Differences: +5, +7, +9, +11
Next difference = +13
35+13 = 48
```

## Type 2 — Second difference

**Example:** `2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?`

```text
First differences: 4, 6, 8, 10
Next difference: 12
Answer: 42
```

## Type 3 — Multiplication

**Example:** `2, 6, 18, 54, ?`

```text
Each term ×3
Answer = 162
```

## Type 4 — Multiply and add

**Example:** `2, 5, 11, 23, ?`

```text
×2+1 each time
23×2+1 = 47
```

## Type 5 — Familiar number families

Look for:

```text
Squares: 1,4,9,16,25,36...
Cubes:   1,8,27,64,125...
Primes:  2,3,5,7,11,13...
Fibonacci: each term = previous two terms added
```

**Example:** `4, 9, 16, 25, ?` → `2²,3²,4²,5²`, so answer `6²=36`.

## Type 6 — Two alternating series

**Example:** `2, 5, 4, 7, 6, 9, ?`

Odd positions: `2,4,6,?` → next `8`.  
Even positions: `5,7,9`.

**Answer: 8.**

## Letter series

Convert letters to positions.

**Example:** `A, C, F, J, O, ?`

```text
Positions: 1,3,6,10,15
Differences: +2,+3,+4,+5
Next: +6 → 21 = U
```

## Opposite-end series

**Example:** `AZ, BY, CX, ?`

- First letters move forward: A, B, C, **D**.
- Second letters move backward: Z, Y, X, **W**.

**Answer: DW.**

## Gap-filled letter series

### Fast method

1. Count the length of a likely repeating block.
2. Divide the line with pencil marks.
3. Complete one block.
4. Check that the same structure works in every block.

**Original example:** `a b _ | a _ c | _ b c`

Each block should be `a b c`, so missing letters are `c, b, a`.

## Common traps

- Checking only addition and missing an alternating series.
- Seeing multiplication but ignoring the extra `+1` or `−1`.
- Converting letters incorrectly after M/N.
- Choosing a rule that fails at an earlier term.
- Filling each blank separately instead of finding the repeated block.

> **20-second recap:** Differences first, then ratios, special numbers, and odd/even positions. For letters, convert to 1–26.

# 3. Analogy and Relationship

## Recognise it

Typical form:

```text
A : B :: C : ?
```

The relationship from A to B must be repeated from C to the answer.

## Type 1 — Meaning relationship

Common relationships:

- Worker : workplace
- Animal : home
- Tool : function
- Young one : adult
- Part : whole
- Country : capital
- Cause : effect

**Example:** `Bird : Nest :: Bee : ?`  
Nest is the conventional nesting place associated with a bird; a hive is the dwelling/colony structure associated with a bee. **Answer: Hive.**

## Type 2 — Number relationship

**Example with two completed pairs:** `8 : 64`, `9 : 81`; therefore `12 : ?`

```text
8² = 64 and 9² = 81
The simplest common rule is n → n²
12² = 144
```

**Answer: 144.** In an MCQ, confirm the rule against every completed pair and the options; one pair alone can allow several accidental rules.

Before accepting a rule, test common forms:

- Square/cube
- `×n ± k`
- Sum/product of digits
- Reverse digits
- Difference between numbers

## Type 3 — Letter relationship

**Example:** `CAT : DBU :: DOG : ?`

Each letter moves +1, so answer = **EPH**.

## Type 4 — Pair-to-pair relationship

When two completed pairs are given before the unknown pair, find the rule common to **both** completed pairs. This removes accidental rules.

## Quick option method

Sometimes it is faster to apply the likely rule to the options than to derive a complicated formula from scratch. Eliminate options that fail even one position.

## Common traps

- Matching meaning when the question uses letter positions.
- Accepting a numeric rule that works for one pair but not another.
- Reversing the direction: the rule is A→B, not B→A.
- Using number of letters unless the question structure supports it.

> **20-second recap:** Say the relationship in one short sentence, then repeat that exact sentence for the second pair.

# 4. Syllogism and Statement–Conclusion

## Recognise it

The question gives statements such as “All A are B,” followed by conclusions. Treat the statements as true even if they sound strange.

## Draw tiny Venn diagrams

This Venn method applies to **categorical** All/No/Some/Only statements. Other statement–conclusion questions require direct analysis of the propositions or evidence.

Use circles; do not rely on real-world knowledge.

```text
All A are B  → A ⊆ B; no A lies outside B
No A is B    → A and B do not overlap
Some A are B → place × in the overlap
Some A not B → place × in A outside B
```

`All A are B` does not say that A exists, that A and B differ, or that B has members outside A.

## Four rules to memorise

1. **All A are B does not mean all B are A.**
2. **No A is B can be reversed:** no B is A.
3. **Some A are B can be reversed:** some B are A.
4. **Some A are not B cannot be reversed.**

Unless the question explicitly specifies another convention, use **no-existential-import** logic:

- `All A are B`, `No A is B`, and `Only A are B` do not by themselves prove that A or B exists.
- `Some A are B` provides at least one member in both A and B.
- `Some A are not B` provides an A-member outside B, but does not prove that any B exists.
- An existential conclusion follows only when a relevant “some” witness is forced through the stated chain into every class named in that conclusion.

## Valid chain patterns

### Pattern 1

```text
All A are B
All B are C
Therefore all A are C
```

### Pattern 2

```text
All A are B
No B is C
Therefore no A is C
```

### Pattern 3

```text
Some A are B
All B are C
Therefore some A are C
```

### Pattern 4

```text
Some A are B
No B is C
Therefore some A are not C
```

## Worked example 1

Statements:

- All pens are tools.
- All tools are useful.

Conclusion: All pens are useful.

```text
Pens ⊆ Tools ⊆ Useful
```

**Conclusion follows.**

## Worked example 2

Statements:

- Some cats are pets.
- No pet is wild.

Conclusion: Some cats are not wild.

The cats marked in the pet circle cannot be wild.  
**Conclusion follows.**

## Worked example 3 — the classic trap

Statements:

- All roses are flowers.
- Some flowers are red.

Conclusion: Some roses are red.

The red flowers may lie outside the rose circle.  
**Conclusion does not follow.**

## Worked example 4

Statements:

- No doctor is careless.
- Some teachers are doctors.

Conclusion: Some teachers are not careless.

The teachers who are doctors cannot be careless.  
**Conclusion follows.**

## Word “only”

For the exact form:

```text
Only A are B = All B are A = B ⊆ A
```

Do not infer `All A are B`, and do not infer existence.

**Example:** “Only graduates are eligible” means all eligible people are graduates. It does not mean every graduate is eligible.

Word order matters: `A are only B` means `All A are B`. Do not apply this shortcut unchanged to phrases such as “only a few A are B.”

## Conclusion-check method

For each conclusion ask:

1. Is it forced in every possible diagram?
2. Did I reverse an “all” statement?
3. Did I create a “some” witness without a relevant “some” premise, or use an unrelated witness?
4. Am I using outside knowledge?

If a conclusion is merely possible but not certain, it does **not** follow in an ordinary definite-conclusion question.

## Common traps

- Reversing “all.”
- Treating “some” as “all.”
- Assuming two groups overlap because both are inside a larger group.
- Using facts from real life.
- Confusing “can be” with “must be.”

> **20-second recap:** Draw circles. A conclusion follows only if every valid diagram forces it.

# 5. Figural / Non-Verbal Reasoning

The archived text layers do not reliably reveal figure subtypes, so these notes teach a general visual method rather than claiming a subtype frequency.

## The SCAN method

Check these four things in order:

1. **S — Shape count:** lines, dots, sides, arrows, enclosed regions.
2. **C — Change:** added, removed, shaded or unshaded.
3. **A — Angle/movement:** rotation direction and number of degrees.
4. **N — Next position:** movement around corners, sides or cells.

## Figure series

Make a tiny table:

| Feature | Figure 1 | Figure 2 | Figure 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rotation from previous figure | — | +90° | +90° |
| Dots | 1 | 2 | 3 |
| Shading | white | black | white |

If Figure 1 starts at 0°, these rotations place Figures 2 and 3 at 90° and 180°; the next transition would add another 90°, giving 270°.

Choose the option that continues **all** changing features, not just one.

## Figure analogy

Ask: what exact operation changes the first figure into the second?

Possible operations:

- Rotate 90°, 180° or 270°
- Reflect left–right or top–bottom
- Add/remove one element
- Move an inner symbol clockwise
- Exchange shading
- Combine or subtract shapes

Apply the same operation to the third figure.

## Mirror and water image

- Reflection in a **vertical mirror line** swaps left and right while retaining top and bottom.
- Reflection in a **horizontal water line** swaps top and bottom while retaining left and right.
- Reflection is not rotation, and it does not change the count of elements.

### Clock mirror shortcut

For a standard analogue clock reflected across the vertical 12–6 axis, let `t` be the number of minutes after 12:00:

```text
Mirror reading = (720 − t) mod 720
```

Display hour 0 as 12. The familiar `11:60 − time` shortcut works only after treating 12 as 0 and normalising the result.

Examples:

```text
3:20  → 8:40
3:00  → 9:00
12:20 → 11:40
11:55 → 12:05
12:00 → 12:00
```

## Paper folding and holes

Undo folds in reverse order. At each crease, reflect only the punch marks carried by the folded flap/layers affected by that fold, and retain only positions on the original sheet.

- An unfold may—but need not—double every visible hole.
- A mark on the crease does not duplicate across that crease.
- Two independent folds can create four positions when all affected layers are punched.

## Embedded/completion figures

- Trace one unusual angle or long line first.
- Ignore extra lines in an embedded-figure option.
- In completion, check line direction at every boundary.

## Common traps

- Tracking only rotation and ignoring dot count or shading.
- Treating a water image as a mirror image.
- Rotating the page mentally in the wrong direction.
- Rejecting an embedded figure because the option has extra lines.
- Counting decorative intersections as separate objects.

> **20-second recap:** Count, compare, track movement, and test every changed feature against the options.

# 6. Blood Relations

## Draw, do not imagine

Use simple symbols:

```text
□ = male
○ = female
— = marriage
│ = parent-child
```

Or write only `M`, `F` and arrows if faster.

## Generation levels

| Level | Relations |
|---:|---|
| +2 | Grandparents |
| +1 | Parents, uncle, aunt |
| 0 | Self, sibling, spouse, cousin |
| −1 | Son, daughter, nephew, niece |
| −2 | Grandchildren |

## Paternal and maternal

- Father's side = paternal.
- Mother's side = maternal.

## Simple chain example

- M is the father of N.
- N is the mother of P.

M is P's **maternal grandfather**.

## Coded relation example

Suppose:

```text
A + B means A is the mother of B
A × B means A is the brother of B
```

Find A's relation to C in `A + B × C`.

```text
A is mother of B.
B is brother of C.
If “brother” is defined—or conventionally intended in this puzzle—to mean a full sibling sharing the same mother, A is mother of C.
Without that convention or an extra premise, A's exact relation to C is not determined.
```

## Pointing questions

Break the sentence from the inside out.

**Example:** A man says, “She is the daughter of the only son of my mother.”

```text
My mother's only son = the speaker
Daughter of speaker = his daughter
```

## “Only” matters

- “Only son” confirms male and excludes other sons; it does not exclude daughters.
- “Only daughter” confirms female and excludes other daughters; it does not exclude sons.
- “Brother's only sister” may be the speaker herself if the speaker is female.

## Unknown gender

Do not assume gender from a letter or name. If gender is not stated, the exact relation may be “sibling,” “parent,” or “child” rather than brother/sister, father/mother, son/daughter.

## Coded-chain method

1. Rewrite every symbol as a full relation.
2. Mark gender immediately.
3. Place each person by generation.
4. Follow the path between the two people asked.
5. Add maternal/paternal only after identifying the connecting parent.

## Common traps

- Starting with the speaker instead of the innermost relation.
- Assuming a spouse is a blood relative.
- Missing gender words.
- Confusing maternal grandfather with paternal grandfather.
- Reading `A is father of B` backwards.

> **20-second recap:** Mark gender and generation, draw each link, then trace only the path asked.

# 7. Ordering, Ranking and Arrangement

The ranking formulas below assume one linear order of `N` people, unique positions with no ties, and the same population counted from both ends.

## Rank from opposite end

For `N` people:

```text
Rank from other end = N − given rank + 1
```

**Example:** Riya is 12th from the left in a row of 30.

```text
From right = 30−12+1 = 19th
```

## Total people from both ranks

If the same person is `L`th from left and `R`th from right:

```text
Total = L + R − 1
```

**Example:** 9th from left and 14th from right:

```text
Total = 9+14−1 = 22
```

## People between two positions

If both ranks are measured from the same end:

```text
People between = |Rank₁ − Rank₂| − 1
```

**Example:** 7th and 18th from left:

```text
18−7−1 = 10 people
```

If ranks come from opposite ends, draw numbered slots unless the order is explicitly clear.

## Dictionary order

Compare one letter at a time from the left.

**Example:** Arrange `Bat, Batch, Bath, Baton`.

- `Bat` is a complete prefix, so it comes first.
- Compare the next letter after `Bat`: `c`, `h`, `o`.

```text
Bat, Batch, Bath, Baton
```

### Dictionary rule

If one word is a complete prefix of another, the shorter word comes first.

## Meaningful order

Find the real sequence: beginning → process → result.

**Example:** Seed, fruit, flower, plant.

```text
Seed → Plant → Flower → Fruit
```

## Simple linear arrangement

1. Draw slots.
2. Place absolute clues first: ends, middle, exact positions.
3. Then place relative clues: left/right, next to, between.
4. Recheck every clue.

### Facing direction

- On a north-up diagram, facing north gives left = west and right = east.
- Facing south gives left = east and right = west.

## Common traps

- Forgetting `−1` because the ranked person is counted from both ends.
- Counting the two named people when asked for people between them.
- Sorting by word length instead of first unequal letter.
- Reversing left/right for a person facing south.

> **20-second recap:** Use `N−r+1`, `L+R−1`, and `difference−1`; draw slots when directions mix.

# 8. Mathematical Operations and Number Logic

## Symbol substitution

When symbols have changed meanings:

1. Substitute every operator simultaneously.
2. Preserve operand order and all original parentheses; do not add brackets that change grouping.
3. Evaluate the replacement operators with normal precedence: multiplication/division left to right, then addition/subtraction left to right.

**Example:** If `+` means `×` and `×` means `−`, evaluate:

```text
8 + 3 × 4
```

Replace signs first:

```text
8 × 3 − 4 = 24−4 = 20
```

## Interchanged signs

If `+` and `−` are interchanged, replace every `+` with `−` and every `−` with `+` simultaneously. Do not change the new symbols again.

A safe trick is to rewrite them on a new line before calculating.

## Equation correction

When asked which signs or numbers should be interchanged:

- Test the easiest-looking option first.
- Rewrite only once per option.
- Use rough size to reject impossible options.
- Apply BODMAS after replacement.

## Missing-number patterns

Check simple row/column rules:

- Sum or difference
- Product
- Square or cube
- Sum of squares
- Product ± a small number
- Same rule across every row

**Example:**

```text
2, 3 → 13
4, 5 → 41
6, 7 → ?
```

Rule:

```text
2²+3² = 13
4²+5² = 41
6²+7² = 36+49 = 85
```

**Answer: 85.**

## Custom operation

If `a ★ b = a² + b`, then:

```text
4 ★ 3 = 4²+3 = 19
```

Use the definition exactly. A custom symbol does not obey ordinary multiplication rules unless stated. Do not assume ordinary precedence or associativity for repeated custom symbols unless the question defines it.

## Common traps

- Calculating before replacing all symbols.
- Ignoring BODMAS after replacement.
- Swapping signs twice.
- Finding a pattern that works for only one row.
- Treating a custom symbol like an ordinary mathematical sign.

> **20-second recap:** Replace first, calculate second. For number logic, demand one simple rule that works everywhere.

# 9. Direction, Distance and Classification

These were lower-frequency in the combined archive but are useful insurance topics.

## Coordinate method for direction

Use an imaginary graph:

```text
North = +y
South = −y
East  = +x
West  = −x
```

## Turn table

| Facing | Left turn | Right turn |
|---|---|---|
| North | West | East |
| East | North | South |
| South | East | West |
| West | South | North |

## Worked example

A person walks 5 m north, turns right and walks 3 m, then turns right and walks 5 m.

```text
Start (0,0)
North 5 → (0,5)
East 3  → (3,5)
South 5 → (3,0)
```

The person is **3 m east** of the start.

## Shortest distance

If `x` and `y` are perpendicular net displacements and the question asks for unrestricted straight-line displacement:

```text
Shortest distance = √(x²+y²)
```

If movement is constrained to roads or permitted segments, calculate the shortest **allowed path** instead.

Remember common triples: `3-4-5`, `5-12-13`, `8-15-17`.

## Classification / odd one out

Find the common property shared by three options.

Possible bases:

- Same category or use
- Same number rule
- Same letter shift
- Squares, cubes or primes
- Same figure rotation or symmetry

**Example:** `8, 27, 64, 100`

```text
8=2³, 27=3³, 64=4³
100 is not a cube
```

**Odd one out: 100.**

## Common traps

- Reporting total path length instead of shortest displacement.
- Using a weak odd-one-out rule when a clearer common rule exists.
- Applying a turn from the page's orientation instead of the person's current heading. A right quarter-turn is always clockwise, but the resulting compass direction depends on the heading at that moment.

> **20-second recap:** Plot movement with coordinates. For classification, state the shared rule of the three matching options.

# Reasoning — final decision tree

When you see a question, identify it before solving:

```text
Coded word/sentence?       → Coding-decoding
Terms with ? or blanks?    → Series
A:B :: C:?                 → Analogy
Categorical All/No/Some/Only? → Venn syllogism
Other statement-conclusion?  → Analyse propositions directly
Only images?               → Figural SCAN method
Family words/symbols?      → Blood relation diagram
Left/right/rank/order?     → Ranking or arrangement
Changed operators?         → Rewrite signs, then BODMAS
Walking/turning?           → Coordinate direction method
Odd option?                → Find common rule of other three
```

## Reasoning attempt order

This is a suggested starting order; change it according to your timed accuracy and the difficulty of the actual questions.

1. Direct analogy, ranking and blood relation.
2. Clear series and coding.
3. Syllogism and operations.
4. Figure questions that show an obvious change.
5. Long or unclear pattern questions last.

## Final ten rules

1. For position-based letter questions, convert letters to positions.
2. Test simple coding rules before complex ones.
3. Split alternating series into odd/even positions.
4. In analogy, repeat the exact relationship.
5. In syllogism, draw circles and accept only forced conclusions.
6. In figures, track count, change, angle and movement.
7. In blood relations, mark gender and generation.
8. In ranking, remember the `+1` and `−1` corrections.
9. In operator questions, replace all signs before BODMAS.
10. Skip and return if the rule is not visible quickly.
